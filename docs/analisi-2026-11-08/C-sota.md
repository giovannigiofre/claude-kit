## 4. Measured evidence

Arxiv was reachable only through search snippets; abstracts and dates below come from `arxiv.org/abs` snippets and secondary summaries, and the specific numbers are marked where they come from a secondary source. Papers dated before 2026-10-29 are included only where they bear on an assumption the kit states, with the kit file:line.

**arXiv 2611.04367, "Do Coding Agents Follow AGENTS.md? The Effect of Policy Wording on Instruction Compliance" (Tsuchida et al., submitted 2026-11-03), https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04367.** One policy was inserted in four wordings into the context files of 86 benchmark tasks across four harness–model combinations. "Conditional phrasing was followed less often than Prescriptive and Explanatory", and which of those two won varied by combination. A secondary summary (emergentmind.com) reports compliance of 71.6 % prescriptive, 49.4 % explanatory, 35.6 % conditional and 17.3 % prohibitive, alongside a second, much lower set (20.4 % / 6.7 % / <1 %) without saying which metric each is — treat the figures as unverified. Bears on `README.md:217-218` ("Explain the why instead of commanding") and on `/kit:learn` writing "conditional rules into skills" (CATALOG row 53): a conditional lesson ("when X, do Y") is the wording least followed, which is one more reason to turn it into a hook or a guard rule.

**arXiv 2611.07166, "Multi-step SWE: Can Coding Agents Keep Building on Their Own Code Over Long Horizons?" (Klavis AI, submitted 2026-11-04), https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07166.** 69 tasks, each a chain of 5–15 dependent steps on one codebase, where each step builds on the agent's earlier code. The strongest of nine frontier models (Claude Opus 5.5 with Claude Code, per the authors' blog) reaches 18.5 % step success (CI about 11.6–25.7); 38 of 69 tasks are unsolved by every model. The authors note the benchmark "does not yet separate accumulated errors from a step's inherent difficulty". Bears on `kit:overnight` ("a fresh session per small step, a commit only when the gate passes", `README.md:30`): the gate between steps is the counter to carried-forward mistakes; the number says long chains still fail more often than not.

**arXiv 2611.07307, "Take It From Here: Evaluating Coding Agents On Project Takeover" (Wang et al., submitted 2026-11-05), https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07307.** ReqMemBench: 349 diagnostic targets, 37 with executable delivery tests; agents receive a long history, a new request and the project state. Agents "struggle to build complete, accurate target requirement states from long histories", and "none of six configurations produced fully correct post-request states" (listing snippet). Supports the kit's channel design (`docs/reference.md:204-207`: "The brief is the whole channel") and `/kit:handoff`: the brief carries the current requirement state, not the conversation.

**arXiv 2610.34577, "Rethinking Verification in Agent Harnesses" (Zhang, Zhu, Lin, Cardie, Ding, Zhou; submitted 2026-10-25; 65 pages), https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34577.** Before the window; included because it bears on CATALOG row 67 / `README.md:10-13` ("explicit verification instructions cause over-verification and should be removed"). 17 verification designs across seven frontier coding models on Terminal-Bench and SWE-bench Verified, in four layers (initial instructions, mid-turn reminders, completion gates, independent review). Findings as stated in the abstract and listing: "Initial instructions and execution-time reminders yield modest, model-dependent average gains"; "completion gates can yield larger gains but may substantially increase cost"; a separate reviewer model in a fresh context offers "a better accuracy-cost tradeoff"; "adding reminders after code edits is the recommended choice". The per-design deltas were not retrievable. Reading for the kit: the fresh-context `kit:verifier` and the deterministic `on_edit`/`stop_check` reminders are the two designs the paper favours; prose verification instructions remain the weak lever.

**arXiv 2610.34491, "What Effort Level Should I Choose? On the Economics of Reasoning Effort in LLM Agents" (Sun et al., submitted 2026-10-25), https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34491; code https://github.com/KaiserWhoLearns/ReasoningEffortLLM.** Before the window; bears on the kit's effort policy (`docs/reference.md:209-215`). On a corrected 44-instance SWE-Bench Pro subset (git history removed, network limited to registries, because the original container exposed the reference fix), "naively choosing the highest level is not only cost-inefficient but also offers no guarantee of the best instance-level performance"; starting at the lowest effort and escalating only after a verified failure "matches or improves on fixed top effort across all models and benchmarks at lower cost for most proprietary models". A secondary write-up gives GPT-5.6-sol's per-task cost rising from $0.27 to $1.24 (4.54×) at max effort (unverified). The kit chooses effort by brief shape and has a gate to detect failure; it does not yet escalate on a failed gate (proposal 3).

**arXiv 2610.35297, "Testing with Coding Agents: A Large-Scale Comparison of Agent-Supported and Human-Authored Pull Requests" (Milanese et al., ≈2026-10-26, "13 days ago" on the listing), https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35297.** 114,134 merged PRs (Jan 2025–Apr 2026) in 391 Java repositories, five agents. Agent-supported PRs add tests more often and modify or delete them less often; "no difference in most structural characteristics of the tests added"; "no difference in coverage of production changes in the 95 repositories that could be built"; "agent tests cover less of the exercised code in test-only PRs"; test-smell differences borderline. Bears on CATALOG row 89 (`init/stacks.md` gate rows justified by "27 % of changed Python lines executed by a test", 2607.18057): the coverage gap is language- and PR-type-dependent; a diff-coverage gate earns its place on Python repos and on test-only PRs, less on Java feature PRs.

**Anthropic, "The components of agent memory" (2026-11-06)** — see section 2 for the numbers (write-rule penalty −0.234 on Opus 5; memory +0.18 on CL-Bench for Opus 5.5; 41/57 vs 57/57 on shared-table vs per-item files). It is the one measured source in the window on instruction style for memory, and it argues against prescriptive write rules for strong models.

Not found in the window: no vendor study (METR, GitClear, HumanLayer SlopCodeBench update) dated on or after 2026-10-29 surfaced; the Microsoft rollout study (arXiv 2607.01418, July 2026, "about 24 % more merged PRs", review coverage down) is older and corrects nothing the kit states. Multi-agent coordination overhead: nothing new since the Sept 2026 "When More Is Less" (2609.19759) already in the kit's evidence base (row 90).
